## 11. City Boy: from memory

~~~text
A City Boy outfit for autumn

The City Boy look is relaxed Japanese prep with a bit of outdoor gear mixed in. Everything is roomy and layered, in muted colours like navy, olive, cream, brown and grey. Fit matters more than any logo. Go up a size on tops, pick trousers that drape wide, and wear everything a little loose.

The pieces

Oversized Oxford button-down. Get it in pale blue, ecru or a soft stripe, untucked or half-tucked. The cheap version is the Uniqlo Oxford shirt sized up, or the Uniqlo U boxy shirts. Mid-range options include Beams Plus or a Polo Ralph Lauren “big fit” Oxford. At the top end, look at Kaptain Sunshine or Auralee.

Knit vest or cardigan. A V-neck sweater vest over the shirt is the most recognisable City Boy layer. Uniqlo and Muji make good cheap ones. Beams, United Arrows and Nanamica are the step up, and Auralee or Batoner if you want really nice yarn.

Wide pleated trousers. Choose chinos or wool trousers that fall straight and full, with a slight break over the shoe. Uniqlo’s wide-fit pleated chinos are the budget pick. Gramicci or Battenwear climbing pants give a more outdoorsy take. Graphpaper and Comoli are the high-end reference points.

Outer layer. This is where the outdoor influence comes in. Good options are a navy chore coat, a nylon field jacket, a fleece, or an oversized waxed jacket. Engineered Garments, Nanamica, Patagonia (the Retro-X fleece is a classic), and Beams’ Barbour collaborations all fit. Uniqlo U usually has a decent chore jacket or parka each season.

Shoes. New Balance in grey suede is the default, especially the 990, 993 or 2002R. Clarks Wallabees, Paraboot Michael, or Birkenstock Bostons with socks are the more grown-up alternatives.

Accessories. Add a nylon six-panel or wool cap, white or coloured ribbed socks, and either an L.L.Bean Boat and Tote or a Porter (Yoshida) Tanker shoulder bag. Round tortoiseshell glasses finish it off if you wear them.

Realistic budgets

These are approximate euro prices from memory, so check current listings.

Tier	Approach	Rough total
Entry	Uniqlo/Muji clothing, Clarks or New Balance 530s, thrifted outerwear	€300–450
Mid	Beams/Polo shirt, Gramicci trousers, Patagonia fleece, NB 2002R, Porter bag	€900–1,300
Premium	Auralee/Graphpaper/Comoli pieces, Engineered Garments jacket, Paraboot or made-in-USA NB	€2,000–3,500+

A smart way to do this is to mix tiers. Spend on the shoes and the jacket, which people notice and which last. Go cheap on shirts and knits.

Where to buy

For the basics, Uniqlo stores and the Uniqlo website cover a lot. For the Japanese and outdoor labels in Europe, multi-brand shops like END. Clothing, Mr Porter and SSENSE stock Auralee, Nanamica, Engineered Garments, Gramicci, Porter and similar brands. Beams and United Arrows have limited direct shipping outside Japan. For those, plus Japanese marketplaces like Zozotown, a proxy service such as Buyee works, but expect EU import duties and VAT on top. Vinted and vintage shops are excellent for Oxfords, knit vests, fleeces and chinos. Much of the look is basically 1980s American prep, which turns up secondhand all the time. Clarks, New Balance and Birkenstock all sell direct online.

Which years it belongs to

The name comes from Popeye, the Japanese men’s magazine founded in 1976. Its early issues celebrated young Tokyo men dressing in American campus and West Coast styles. In 2012 the magazine relaunched under editor Takahiro Kinoshita with the tagline “Magazine for City Boys.” That’s when the modern version of the look took shape, with its oversized prep, outdoor gear and thoughtful layering. It became a global aesthetic around 2022–2024, mainly through TikTok and Instagram, when the “City Boy outfit” format spread far beyond Japan.

So the honest answer is that it belongs to 2012 onward in Japan, with its international peak around 2022–2024. By late 2026 the viral moment has likely cooled, though I can’t confirm current trends without checking. The good news is that the clothes themselves are durable classics, so a well-built version of this outfit won’t look dated once the hashtag fades.
~~~

## 11. City Boy: with the record

~~~text
City Boy Outfit

Era: 2012 to present. The look took shape when Popeye magazine relaunched in 2012 with the tagline “Magazine for City Boys” and an editorial vision of roomy, relaxed American prep and outdoor wear filtered through Tokyo taste. It’s still going strong, and it has spread globally through brands like Auralee and Comoli. It isn’t slim-fit Ivy: everything is cut generously and allowed to drape and slouch.

The Outfit
Shirt: Oversized oxford button-down. Beams Plus is the classic choice: a heavy oxford with a soft collar roll, sized up one. For a more refined, drapier version, Comoli’s cotton shirts are the high end of the look. Wear it untucked or loosely half-tucked.
Trousers: Wide-leg chinos. Auralee’s wide chinos and Nanamica’s chino pants both have the right full, straight leg with a little break or a cropped hem. Khaki, olive, or ecru work best.
Layer: Unstructured navy blazer or chore-style jacket. A soft-shouldered, unlined navy blazer from Beams Plus or Comoli dresses the look up. For a more casual outdoor lean, a Nanamica GORE-TEX cruiser jacket or soutien collar coat fits the City Boy mix of prep and gear.
Shoes: New Balance 990s. Grey is the default. Paraboot Michaels or Clarks Wallabees are good alternatives if you want a loafer-ish feel.
Extras: White crew socks, a canvas tote or Nautica JP shoulder bag, and maybe a plain 6-panel cap. Keep accessories minimal and functional.
Where to Buy

New: SSENSE carries Auralee and other Japanese labels, and Haven is a strong specialist for Nanamica, Auralee, and similar brands. Japanese brands also often sell more cheaply on their own sites or through Japanese retailers that ship internationally.

Secondhand: Yahoo Auctions Japan is the best source for Beams Plus, Comoli, and Nanamica at good prices. You’ll need a proxy service such as Buyee or ZenMarket. Mercari Japan, through the same proxies, and Grailed are also worth checking.

Sizing tip: Go up one size in tops, and choose trousers with a genuinely wide cut rather than sizing up slim ones. Sizing up slim trousers just makes them baggy in the wrong places.
~~~

## 12. Frutiger Aero Fashion: with the record

~~~text
Frutiger Aero Outfit

Era: roughly 2004–2013, the years of Windows Vista and 7 glassy interfaces, the Wii, glossy bubble icons, and stock photos of water droplets on green leaves.

One honest caveat first. “Frutiger Aero” is a retroactive label, coined around 2017 by the Consumer Aesthetics Research Institute. It describes graphic and UI design more than clothing. Nobody in 2007 called their outfit Frutiger Aero, so a “Frutiger Aero fashion” look is a modern reconstruction built from the clothing of that period that matches the visual mood.

The Outfit

1. Outer layer: translucent or glossy technical shell (Nike ACG)
Look for a vintage Nike ACG shell in white, ice blue, or pale aqua, ideally with a slightly shiny or semi-sheer nylon finish. Mid-2000s ACG pieces with taped seams and ergonomic panelling hit the “tech-optimism” note well. A light iridescent windbreaker works as an alternative.

2. Eyewear: aqua- or blue-tinted shield sunglasses (Oakley)
Choose vintage Oakley wraparounds with ice- or sapphire-iridium lenses. The Half Jacket, Racing Jacket, Radar, and Eye Jacket all fit, especially in white or silver frames. Skip the dark, aggressive X-Metal styles, which read more as menace than optimism.

3. Base layer: glossy synthetic top
A white or aqua mid-2000s Nike Dri-FIT or similar technical tee, or a lightweight zip mock-neck, keeps everything clean and bright.

4. Bottoms: light technical pants
White, pale grey, or light blue nylon track pants or slim technical cargos. Nike ACG or Prada Linea Rossa nylon trousers are the dream version.

5. Sneakers: ergonomic, bubble-sole runners
Nike Air Max 360 (2006), Air Max 2009, or Nike Shox in white, blue, and silver colourways. The visible air bubble in the Air Max is about as Frutiger Aero as footwear gets. Nike Free (2004 onward) is a subtler option.

6. Accessories
A Prada Linea Rossa nylon crossbody or shoulder bag in white or silver. A translucent Swatch or a clear or ice-blue Casio Baby-G watch. Bonus points for wired white earbuds.

eBay and Depop are the main sources, especially for Oakley and Nike vintage. Grailed is good for Prada Linea Rossa and higher-end ACG. Vinted often has cheaper Nike tech pieces. Search with era terms like “Y2K,” “2000s,” “vintage ACG,” or “Oakley ice iridium,” and check sneaker sizing carefully, since old foam and air units can degrade.

Notes on the Reference Record

The dataset is mostly sound, but two points are worth flagging:
~~~

## 13. Terrace Casuals: from memory

~~~text
Terrace Casuals Outfit
The era

Terrace casual style is a British subculture that grew out of football grounds. It began around 1977–78, when Liverpool fans following their club through European competitions came home with continental sportswear that nobody in the UK had seen. It spread to Manchester and London clubs by about 1980 and peaked between roughly 1981 and 1985. Lads competed to wear the rarest Italian tracksuit or the most obscure Adidas trainer.

The look changed after that. The 1985 ban on English clubs in Europe cut off the supply line, and acid house pushed fashion baggier in 1988–89. Through the 1990s casuals moved toward Stone Island and C.P. Company. A 2000s revival followed, helped by films like The Football Factory and Green Street, and it never really went away.

The outfit below is a circa 1982–84 classic, with a swap if you’d rather go the 90s route.

The scene’s reputation is tangled up with 80s football hooliganism. Today the look reads mostly as heritage menswear, and it’s popular well beyond football grounds.
~~~

## 13. Terrace Casuals: with the record

~~~text
Terrace Casuals Outfit

Era: Late 1970s to early 1990s. The look began on the Liverpool and Manchester terraces around 1977–79, when fans brought back European sportswear from away trips. It peaked in the mid-80s and blurred into the acid house and Madchester scene by the early 90s.

The rule: no team colours, scarves or replica shirts. The point was to look sharp and expensive, not to show allegiance. That’s what separates it from Blokecore.

The Outfit
Jacket: C.P. Company goggle jacket. The Mille Miglia hooded jacket, with lenses built into the hood, is the most recognisable casual outerwear. For something lower-key, a Stone Island overshirt or field jacket with the compass badge on the left sleeve also works.
Knit: Stone Island crewneck. Wear the badge visible on the arm. For an earlier late-70s/early-80s feel, use a Pringle or Lyle & Scott diamond-pattern jumper instead.
Track top (alternative layer): Sergio Tacchini or Fila. A zip-up track top from Tacchini, or a Fila BJ in navy, white or burgundy, captures the early-80s tennis-wear phase. You can wear it under the jacket or in place of the knit.
Trousers: straight-leg jeans or cords. Faded straight jeans or cords in a muted colour, never baggy or skinny. Lois was the period favourite.
Trainers: Adidas city series. München, Dublin, Stockholm and similar models in suede and gum soles. Trimm Trab, Forest Hills and Samba are also correct.
Where to Buy

Vintage and secondhand: eBay, Vinted and Grailed are the best sources for C.P. Company, Stone Island, Tacchini and Fila. Vinted is especially good for knitwear and track tops at low prices. Check C.P. and Stone Island listings carefully, because fakes are common. Look at the badge stitching, the authenticity tags (on newer pieces) and the seller’s history.

New: Adidas regularly reissues city-series and Spezial models, so you don’t need fragile vintage pairs. Stone Island and C.P. Company still sell their signature pieces new, at full designer prices.

Tacchini/Fila track top	$50–150	$100–150
Jeans/cords	$30–80	$80–150
Adidas city-series trainers	$60–120	$100–140

One small note on the reference record: The Jam are more of a Mod-revival band than a casuals band. If you want a soundtrack, the Stone Roses and Happy Mondays are the stronger match for the late-era look.
~~~

## 14. Amekaji: from memory

~~~text
Autumn Amekaji Outfit

Era: Amekaji (American casual) is a Japanese reinterpretation of mid-century American clothing. The garments copy American workwear, military, and campus clothes from roughly the 1930s to the 1960s. The Japanese movement has several phases. Ivy style arrived in the 1960s through VAN Jacket and the 1965 photobook Take Ivy. In the mid-1970s, Popeye magazine and the Made in USA catalog pushed an outdoor and heritage look. It became a full street style in the late 1980s–90s, through Shibuya casual and the vintage Levi’s frenzy, when the “Osaka Five” denim brands emerged. It reached Western menswear in a big way around 2008–2015 with the heritage boom and W. David Marx’s book Ametora (2015). It never really left: the core brands today are the same ones that defined the look 25–30 years ago.

The outfit
Jeans: Warehouse Lot 1001XX or Full Count 1101 (~€230–300). Both are raw selvedge reproductions of 1940s–50s Levi’s. Wear them slightly roomy with a gentle taper, and cuff once or twice so the selvedge shows. The Flat Head and Momotaro are good alternatives if you want a slubbier, more textured denim.
Base layer: Whitesville or Warehouse pocket tee (~€50–70 for a two-pack or single). These are tubular-knit, heavyweight cotton tees modelled on 1950s undershirts. Get white or grey.
Shirt: heavy flannel from UES or Buzz Rickson’s chambray (~€150–220). Wear it open over the tee, or buttoned under the jacket on colder days.
Mid-layer: Warehouse or The Real McCoy’s set-in-sleeve sweatshirt (~€150–220). Choose heather grey. Loopwheeled cotton with V-gussets at the neck is the classic detail.
Jacket: Buzz Rickson’s N-1 deck jacket (~€700–900). This is a museum-grade reproduction of the WWII navy jacket, in navy or khaki jungle cloth with alpaca lining. It’s ideal for October through December. A cheaper alternative is a Type II or Type III denim jacket from the same brand as your jeans (~€250–350).
Boots: Red Wing 875 Moc Toe or 8111 Iron Ranger (~€330–400). The moc toe is the more casual, quintessentially Amekaji option. For a lighter-weather version, Converse Chuck 70s (~€90) are period-correct.
Belt: a thick (38–40mm) saddle-leather belt with a plain brass or steel buckle (~€80–150). Tanner Goods is a reliable choice.

Total for the full outfit is about €1,900–2,400.

Realistic budget tiers
Entry (~€400–650): Uniqlo selvedge jeans or Levi’s, Uniqlo U heavy tees, a vintage flannel, a secondhand denim jacket, and used Red Wings.
Mid (~€1,100–1,500): Japanese selvedge jeans, Whitesville tees, a sweatshirt, a denim jacket instead of the N-1, and new Red Wings.
Full (~€1,900–2,500): everything in the main outfit above.
Where to buy

European specialists: Tenue de Nîmes (Amsterdam), Burg & Schild (Berlin), and Clutch Café and Son of a Stag (London). If you’re buying from the UK into the EU, expect customs fees and import VAT since Brexit, so factor in roughly 20–25% on top.

US stockists: Self Edge and Division Road carry deep ranges of the Japanese brands, but transatlantic shipping and duties add up.

Direct from Japan: brand webshops or Japanese retailers often have lower base prices. Proxy services like Buyee or ZenMarket get you into Yahoo Auctions and Mercari Japan, which are excellent for barely-worn pieces at a fraction of retail. Watch out for import duties and for Japanese sizing, which runs smaller.

Secondhand in Europe: Vinted, Grailed, and eBay are reliable for Red Wings, flannels, and denim. Search exact model numbers like “Warehouse 1001” or “Red Wing 875.”

My prices come from what I knew up to mid-2026 and from typical EU retail. Exchange rates, especially the weak yen, and brand price hikes may have shifted things, so check current listings before budgeting precisely.
~~~

## 14. Amekaji: with the record

~~~text
An Amekaji Outfit

Here’s a classic, wearable Amekaji fit built on a mid-century American look: heavy selvedge denim, a loopwheel sweatshirt, a military flight jacket, and engineer boots. Every piece comes from a Japanese brand. American heritage makers like Red Wing or Wesco belong to Heritage Workwear, a separate aesthetic.

The pieces
Jeans: Iron Heart 634S or Studio D’Artisan SD-103. Iron Heart’s 21oz selvedge is famously heavy and fades dramatically. Studio D’Artisan, one of the original “Osaka Five” repro denim brands, offers a more traditional 50s-style cut. Wear them raw and cuffed to show the selvedge. Expect about $250–400 new.
Sweatshirt: The Real McCoy’s loopwheel crewneck in heather grey. It’s knitted slowly on vintage loopwheel machines, which gives it a dense, soft fabric with no side seams. Look for the V-insert at the neck. Expect about $200–280.
Jacket: Buzz Rickson’s MA-1 or N-1 deck jacket. Buzz Rickson’s makes some of the most accurate reproductions of US military flight gear, down to the contract labels and period hardware. The MA-1 is the easier entry point. The N-1 is warmer and more distinctive. Expect about $500–800. If you want to stretch, a horsehide A-2 from The Real McCoy’s or Buzz Rickson’s is the grail piece, but it often runs $1,500 or more.
Boots: Japanese-made engineer boots, such as Clinch from Brass Tokyo. These are built to vintage engineer patterns with serious leather and construction. They’re expensive, often near $1,000+, so this is where many people buy secondhand.
Accent: a Kapital bandana or belt. Kapital, from Kojima, Japan’s denim capital, adds a looser, more artisanal and slightly eccentric touch to the strict reproduction look. A bandana at around $60–100 is the cheapest way in.

Underneath, a heavyweight pocket tee from The Real McCoy’s or Studio D’Artisan finishes the fit.

Budget

Bought new, this outfit lands at roughly $2,000–3,000, depending mostly on the jacket and boots. Individual pieces generally fall in the $250–1,500 range. Buying secondhand can bring the total closer to $1,000–1,500, because repro denim and flight jackets hold up well and appear on resale markets often. If you import directly from Japan, budget extra for shipping, customs duties, and VAT. These can add a meaningful chunk to the price. All figures here are approximate, so check current prices before you buy.

Where to buy

Yahoo Auctions Japan has the deepest selection and the best prices, especially for boots and jackets. You’ll need a proxy service like Buyee or ZenMarket, since most sellers don’t ship internationally. Grailed is easier for Western buyers and has a solid stream of used Iron Heart, Real McCoy’s, and Buzz Rickson’s. For new pieces with guaranteed sizing and authenticity, use the official brand sites. Specialist denim retailers that stock these labels are another good option.

The era

Amekaji as a distinct aesthetic belongs to the late 1980s to the present. Its roots go back further: Japan’s fascination with American Ivy and outdoor clothing grew through the 60s and 70s, and Studio D’Artisan was founded at the end of the 70s. But the movement took off in the late 80s and 90s, when Japanese reproduction denim and vintage culture boomed. Brands like The Real McCoy’s and Buzz Rickson’s started then, and Iron Heart followed in the 2000s. It’s still very much alive today, with these makers producing new runs every season.
~~~

## 15. Gyaru: from memory

~~~text
A Classic Shibuya Gyaru Outfit (Mid-2000s Style)

When gyaru happened: Gyaru began with the kogal schoolgirl look of the early-to-mid 1990s, went extreme with ganguro and manba (deep tans, white makeup) around 1998–2004, and spread into many substyles through the mid-to-late 2000s, such as onee-gyaru, hime-gyaru and agejo. Magazines like egg, Popteen and Koakuma Ageha defined each era, and Shibuya 109 was the center of it all. The style faded in the early 2010s (egg stopped print in 2014). Since the late 2010s there’s been a real revival, so wearing it in 2026 reads as a deliberate Heisei-nostalgia look, not costume.

The outfit below is a mid-2000s Shibuya gal look, the most recognizable version.

The Pieces

Top: A fitted off-shoulder knit or a rhinestone-detailed cami from Cecil McBee, the most iconic 109 brand. D.I.A. works for something more glam and animal print. It should be tight, short, and a little sparkly.

Bottom: A low-rise denim micro-mini or distressed shorts from Sly, Moussy or Rady. Moussy and Sly were the denim names of the era.

Outer layer: A faux-fur-trimmed hooded jacket or a cropped fur bolero. Moussy, Delyle Noir and Cecil McBee all made these, and fur trim is peak 2000s gal.

Shoes: Knee-high or slouchy platform-heel boots, or platform sandals in summer. Esperanza was the go-to gal shoe brand. Height is the point.

Bag: A Samantha Thavasa handbag, ideally a Samantha Vega. It’s the gyaru status bag, ideally covered in charms.

Hair and face: Big, voluminous curls in lightened brown or honey blonde, with clip-in extensions for length. Add heavy false lashes (Dolly Wink, the line by gal icon Tsubasa Masuwaka, is the classic), circle lenses, a strong lower lash line, pale glossy lips, and decorated nails. Finish with oversized hoops and rhinestone jewelry.

Where to Buy (from Italy)

Most of these pieces are easiest to find secondhand in Japan. Mercari Japan and Yahoo! Auctions Japan have huge stocks of Cecil McBee, Sly, Rady and Samantha Thavasa. You buy through a proxy service like Buyee, ZenMarket or FromJapan, and bundling items into one shipment saves a lot on postage. Vinted and Depop sometimes have pieces in Europe with no import hassle, but less selection. If you’re ever in Tokyo, Shibuya 109 still exists, though the brand lineup has changed a lot. Moussy, Sly, EMODA and GYDA (the modern gal-adjacent brands) sell new online. Brand availability shifts, so check that a label is still trading before planning around buying it new. Dolly Wink lashes and circle lenses are easy to find online. For lenses, buy from a reputable seller and ideally get an optician’s check first.
~~~

## 15. Gyaru: with the record

~~~text
A classic Shibuya Gyaru outfit (late-’90s to 2000s)

Gyaru belongs roughly to 1995–2014. It grew out of the early-’90s kogal schoolgirl look and peaked in the late ’90s and 2000s around the Shibuya 109 mall. It faded in the 2010s, and the 2014 end date lines up with the closure of egg, the scene’s defining magazine. The style never fully died, though, and has had a revival since the late 2010s.

The outfit below is the tanned, high-glam Shibuya version: hibiscus prints, leopard, and platforms.

The pieces
Hibiscus-print mini dress (Alba Rosa). This is the centerpiece. Alba Rosa’s tropical hibiscus prints are probably the single most recognizable gyaru motif. Look for bright pinks, oranges, or turquoise.
Leopard-print top or shrug (Cecil McBee). Cecil McBee was a 109 staple. A leopard cardigan or fitted top can go over the dress or replace it with a denim mini skirt.
Platform boots. Tall, chunky, and ideally white, pink, or leopard. Generic Japanese or Y2K platforms work fine, since the silhouette matters more than the label.
Loose socks (optional). These are the signature kogal item. Wear them with a school-uniform-style skirt if you want the earlier mid-’90s look rather than the 2000s glam version.
Hair, tan, and makeup. These do half the work. You need bleached or honey-blonde hair with volume, a bronzed tan (self-tanner is fine), dramatic eyeliner, heavy false lashes, pale or white highlight, and glossy lips. Add a hibiscus hair clip, chunky jewelry, and long decorated nails.

One note on brands: Liz Lisa and MA*RS are genuine gyaru-associated brands, but they lean toward the sweeter, princess-like Hime Gyaru and Agejo looks. Lace, ruffles, and pastels from those brands would clash with the tanned hibiscus look above. Pick one substyle and stick to it.

Where to buy
Mercari Japan and Yahoo Auctions Japan have the best selection of authentic vintage Alba Rosa and Cecil McBee. If you’re outside Japan, you’ll need a proxy service such as Buyee, ZenMarket, or FromJapan. Proxies add service fees and international shipping, and you may owe import duties on arrival.
Depop is easier and often has Y2K gyaru pieces from Western sellers. Prices are sometimes higher for branded items, but you avoid proxy fees.
Shibuya 109, if you’re ever in Tokyo, is the style’s home, though today’s stock is more modern than vintage.
~~~
